# References

Sources behind the `research-talk` skill. This file is for human readers; the
skill does not load it.

## Sources

- Simon Peyton Jones. _How to give a great research talk_. Talk and slides,
  Microsoft Research; based on a 1993 paper with John Hughes and John
  Launchbury. <https://simon.peytonjones.org/great-research-talk/>,
  slides: <https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/How-to-give-a-great-research-talk.pdf>.
  Primary source. Paper as beef, talk as advertisement; motivation 20%, key
  idea 80%, no third part; narrow and deep; examples as the main weapon; no
  outline, no related work, no apologies; finish on time.
- Patrick Winston. _How to Speak_. MIT OpenCourseWare, 2018.
  <https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/>.
  Empowerment promise instead of an opening joke; cycling, fences around the
  idea, and verbal punctuation; few words per slide, no laser pointer;
  collaborators on the first slide; end on a contributions slide, not "thank
  you"; practice with people who do not know the work.
- Mark D. Hill. _Oral Presentation Advice_. University of Wisconsin–Madison.
  <https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~markhill/conference-talk.html>.
  Talk template with slide counts, about two minutes per slide, outline slide,
  related work superficial or omitted, backup slides, practice in public.
- Michael Alley and Kathryn A. Neeley. _Rethinking the Design of Presentation
  Slides: A Case for Sentence Headlines and Visual Evidence_. Technical
  Communication 52(4), 2005. <http://writing.engr.psu.edu/2005_alley_neeley.pdf>.
  The assertion-evidence slide: a sentence headline stating the point, visual
  evidence instead of bullets.

## Where the sources disagree

The skill takes Peyton Jones's side in each case, with the exceptions noted.

- **Outline slide.** Peyton Jones: no outline at the start, since it conveys
  almost nothing and spends the audience's first two minutes; perhaps one
  after the motivation, plus signposts. Hill: give an outline, because
  audiences like predictability. Winston: announce the outline verbally and
  enumerate the parts, so people who drift off can get back on. _Skill:_ no
  opening outline; an outline after the motivation in long talks; signposts at
  the transitions (which is Winston's verbal punctuation).
- **Bullets and slide titles.** Peyton Jones's own slides use short bulleted
  phrases; he objects only to revealing them one by one. Alley argues against
  bullets altogether: a full-sentence headline plus visual evidence. Winston
  strips slides down to a few words and even drops titles. _Skill:_ few words,
  short phrases allowed; assertion-evidence when the user prefers it.
- **Preparation.** Peyton Jones: write, or at least polish, the slides the
  night before, so the talk is fresh. Hill and Winston stress rehearsal in
  front of others, and Winston specifically with people who do not know the
  work. The two are compatible; the skill recommends both.
- **Questions during the talk.** Peyton Jones encourages questions during the
  talk and would rather truncate than lose the connection. Hill plans for
  questions at the end, with backup slides; many conference sessions also hold
  questions to the end. _Skill:_ invite questions during the talk when the
  format allows it, and prepare backup slides either way.
- **Related work.** Peyton Jones: do not present it, but know it well. Hill:
  cover it superficially or omit it. Winston: "build a fence" around the idea
  by contrasting it with the nearest alternative. _Skill:_ no survey, one
  contrasting sentence where it sharpens the idea.
- **The final slide.** Peyton Jones does not prescribe one. Hill ends with a
  summary slide; Winston insists on a contributions slide and warns against
  "thank you" and "questions?" slides. _Skill:_ follows Winston.

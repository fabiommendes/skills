# Research talk

A talk is not the paper read aloud. The paper is the beef; the talk is the
advertisement for it. The talk's purpose is to give the audience an intuitive
feel for one idea and make them eager to read the paper — not to show
everything you know or every technical detail. The approach follows Simon
Peyton Jones's "How to give a great research talk"; where other guides
disagree with him, his position is the default.

Plan for the audience you get, not the one you want: they have never heard of
you, do not know your theory, and have just had lunch.

## Before planning

Get from the user, and ask for what is missing:

- The time slot, including questions.
- The audience: specialists, a broad field, a general audience, or a hiring
  committee.
- The slide tool: Beamer, reveal.js, Marp, PowerPoint, Keynote, or another.
- The paper or material the talk is about.

## Structure

**Motivation: 20%.** In the first two minutes, answer what the audience is
thinking: why should I listen, what is the problem, why is it interesting, is
this a worthwhile advance? Open with that promise, not with a joke or an
outline.

**Key idea: 80%.** One idea, stated so specifically that you could say "If you
remember nothing else, remember this." Organize the talk around it and prune
everything that does not serve it.

- Narrow and deep beats wide and shallow. Cut to the technical meat. Covering
  only part of the paper is fine; refer to the paper for the rest.
- Examples are the main weapon: to motivate, to convey the intuition, to show
  the idea in action, to show extreme cases, and to show shortcomings. When
  time is short, cut the general case, not the example.
- Leave out dense notation and proofs. Put them in backup slides for
  questions.

**There is no third part.** End on a slide that lists the contributions and
mirrors the opening promise. It stays up during questions, so it carries
content, not "Thank you" or "Questions?". (Winston)

**Orientation.** Skip the outline slide at the start: it carries almost no
information and spends the two minutes the audience grants. In a long talk,
show a short outline after the motivation, and signpost the transitions.

**Related work.** Do not survey it. Contrast your idea with its nearest
alternative in a sentence where that sharpens the idea. Credit co-authors on
the title slide and precursors as you go. Praise the competition.

## Slides

- One point per slide. Plan by time, not slide count; about two minutes per
  slide is a common average. (Hill)
- Few words. The audience can read or listen, not both; slides support what the
  speaker says.
- Large fonts, no logos or background clutter. Prefer pictures, diagrams, and
  code examples to text.
- Short phrases and bullets are fine. Reveal bullets one by one only for a
  punch line. Use animation very sparingly.
- Write speaker notes for every slide with what to say, and script the first
  few sentences of the talk word for word.
- Prepare backup slides for expected questions: details, proofs, and
  shortcomings.

If the user prefers assertion-evidence slides, give each slide a full-sentence
headline stating its point, and support it with visual evidence instead of
bullets. (Alley)

## Planning a talk from a paper

1. State the key idea and the one example that carries it. Get the user's
   agreement before writing slides.
2. Budget the minutes: motivation, the parts of the key idea, contributions.
   The budget must fit the slot with time for questions.
3. List what the talk leaves out of the paper: the related-work survey, proofs,
   the general case, secondary results. Show the list to the user.
4. Write the slides and speaker notes in the user's tool.
5. Review the talk with the steps below.

## Reviewing a talk

Report findings by slide number, in this order:

1. **Key idea:** is there one, and is it stated explicitly?
2. **Motivation:** does it land within two minutes? Does the talk open with an
   outline or a survey instead?
3. **Pruning:** material that does not serve the key idea; breadth where depth
   belongs; notation the audience cannot absorb.
4. **Examples:** is the idea shown with an example before the general case?
5. **Slides:** word count, clutter, font size, one-by-one reveals, animation.
6. **Ending:** a contributions slide, not "Thank you" or "Questions?".
7. **Timing:** does the budget fit the slot?

## Advice for the speaker

When the talk is ready, give the user these reminders:

- Practice in front of people who do not know the work; your advisor and
  close colleagues fill gaps without noticing.
- Polish the slides the night before, so the talk is fresh in your mind.
- Show enthusiasm: if you are not excited by the idea, the audience will not be.
- Do not apologise for the preparation, the results, or the time.
- Face the audience, not the screen. Speak to the back of the room.
- Invite questions during the talk; they are a chance to connect. Cut material
  rather than rush.
- Finish on time, without fail. Truncate and conclude rather than ask to go on.
- Test the projector in advance, and keep a backup copy of the slides.
